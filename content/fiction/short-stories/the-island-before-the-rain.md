*An alternate-history horror story set in 2019*

On the sixth of November, 2019, the last crow on Kurohama flew into a clear sky and struck something hard enough to break its neck.

Mina Sato saw it happen from the ferry. She kept her eye on the place where it had fallen, expecting a ripple. None came. There was a line in the water as straight as the edge of a sheet of paper. On her side the sea heaved. On the island side it held the gray reflection of the sky without a wrinkle.

Her brother had called from Kurohama two nights before. “There’s no food,” he’d said. Then, after a silence she could still measure by the three clicks of her kitchen clock, “I mean someone has taken away the possibility of food.”

The ferryman cut the engine just short of the line.

“You can walk from here,” he said.

Mina took that for a refusal until she looked down and saw a concrete causeway beneath the surface. It had been low tide when they left the mainland. Now the island was letting the water out.

Kurohama had once been a fishing island south of the Izu Peninsula. Its last permanent households had left twelve years earlier. A marine acoustics institute occupied a former school, and a second laboratory had been built into the ridge behind it. A woman from the mainland tended the cemetery. Now the cemetery stones stood in clean black gravel. Even the lichen was gone.

In the school kitchen, an open bread bag lay beside a knife. The bag bulged in the shape of a loaf. When Mina touched it, the plastic collapsed. She opened a refrigerator and found egg cartons holding nothing, a butter wrapper folded around air, and a strip of butcher paper with a price sticker on it. She shut the door gently. Haru had been telling her what he could see.

The duty ledger on the stairs listed twenty-three people. It had only one entry for November fourth. Her brother had signed out a wheelchair at 2:12 a.m.

She found it on the playing field, empty, its wheels sunk in the cinder track. Beyond it lay three ships.

They were long and low, with black keels and high, sloping decks. The nearest had come down across the baseball diamond. Its belly pulsed every few seconds. She could see boards inside the split hull, scored with tally marks, and behind the boards the flank of something with three parallel rows of teeth along its ribs. The hold smelled of hot pennies. A hook the size of an anchor held the carcass in place.

“Please move away from that.”

The voice came from the dugout. A man sat on the bench with a silver sword across his knees. His hair was the same dull silver, tangled where it caught on the ridges of his shoulders. His skin had the white of the underside of a mushroom. As she approached, two narrow slit eyes blinked independently. His cheeks had been broken and drawn together around a little cage of wire. One forearm bent twice between wrist and elbow.

“Where’s my brother?”

“He put me in that chair,” the man said. “I hadn’t been allowed to sit for a long time.”

“Where is he?”

“Below us. I stayed near the chair because I thought he might need it back.”

His name was Ithuriel. He had belonged to the destroying angels, who carried silver swords when Heaven went to war. He had fought in Heaven, above the burning galleries of Hell, and in the cold between planets, where the dead took years to drift out of sight. Silver kept its edge in all three places. On Earth, he told her, it dulled as soon as you used it to cut bread. One of his company had discovered this while breaking a week’s fast in a shepherd’s hut. The sword had been useless ever after, and the angel still kept it.

“How many of you came?”

“Seven. We were taken at a place in the dark where the sun looks no bigger than the other stars. They set our bones to fit their harnesses. They worked on our eyes until we could read orders with the lids closed. Mine still do, sometimes.”

He looked down as he said it.

Satan had sent all seven to Earth with orders to destroy mankind and bring whatever remained of God back to Hell. Mina waited for some account of what could remain of God. Ithuriel seemed embarrassed to have told her that much.

“Why here?” she asked.

“Because there were laboratories. And because I could see the sea.”

The laboratory door below the field had a window of thick glass. Through it Mina could see Haru sitting cross-legged at the bottom of the stairs, eating something from his cupped hand. She beat on the glass until he looked up. He smiled mildly, the way he did whenever he had lost track of how late it was and found her waiting outside a restaurant.

Ithuriel eased the door open with the tip of his sword.

Haru was eating grains of salt. His lips were bleeding. He offered some to Mina before he seemed to recognize her. Then he swept them off his palm and said, “Did you bring the plums?”

She had brought a thermos and two rice balls. The rice balls were gone from the bag. The thermos still felt heavy.

“Who else is here?” she asked.

“Everyone went down to the dock. There was someone on the radio saying the ferry was coming.”

“Who was on the radio?”

Haru turned to Ithuriel, who said, “I was. I can do your voice too, now that I’ve heard it. I’m sorry. They gave the order before I knew any of your names.”

Mina poured water into the thermos cap. Ithuriel backed away.

“There’s no water in Hell,” he said.

“Can you cross it?”

“We crossed all that sky. It was very easy to mistake the two.”

A fluorescent light flickered awake farther down the corridor. Through a window Mina saw a room she recognized from her brother’s photographs. The institute used to bring schoolchildren there to listen to whale calls.

Lengths of silver now hung from the ceiling on wires. The biggest piece was a blade with a thumbprint in it, frozen in the metal. Haru told her what the other angels had made in that room. They’d called it the hollow fever. It had come out of the holds with the meat, and in the laboratory they had given it something it had never had in Hell. They had given it a human name. The name belonged to the first woman who had died of it. Haru had heard her calling for her son all the way from the dock.

“Has it reached the mainland?” Mina asked.

Haru said, “My phone rang this morning. It was the woman who looks after the graves. She asked me why she could hear her husband in the kitchen.”

Mina put the cap back on the thermos. The woman’s husband had been dead for fifteen years.

Haru took Mina’s sleeve. “He chose the island,” he said. “Ithuriel. He told me before they put him back on the ship. The ships have enough life in them to come down once. That’s why there’s meat aboard. They’re feeding their way here and they’ve eaten through everything on land.”

“The canal under the lab fills with the tide,” he said. “They shut it when they came down. The emergency gate is out by the chair.”

“Then we go together.”

Haru looked at their feet. His shadow stretched down the passage toward the shifting doors. Hers lay behind her, where the light put it. He moved his right hand, and the shadow lifted its left.

“I got as far as the track last time,” he said. “It pulled me all the way back.”

At the far end of the passage, someone tried the handles of the double doors. Then the handles stopped moving.

Mina gave him the thermos. She took his hand and sat for a few seconds beside him on the stairs. He turned the red cap slowly between his fingers. Their father had owned a thermos like this, and the last time the three of them had gone out together he had brought barley tea to the cemetery. Haru had spilled most of his serving without noticing because he had been trying to catch a grasshopper for her.

“Are you sure that was me?” he said. She hadn’t realized she’d told him the story out loud.

“Yes.”

“I remember it too.”

He kept hold of the thermos when she got up.

At the playing field it had begun to rain. A few drops hit the cinders and the air smelled of dust again. The crows were gone, and the rain sounded louder in their absence. The three hulls sagged on the field. The meat in the nearest hold let out a thin, sustained cry. It reminded Mina of a kettle from another room.

Ithuriel came as far as the wheelchair. He spread his coat over his head, careful to keep the rain off the sword.

“Will God come for you?” she asked.

He examined the question with a small frown. “I don’t know. I used to think I’d recognize the sound.”

She found the gate winch under a square steel grate. The release wheel wouldn’t turn. Her brother had spent the summer complaining about it. She’d told him that if he hated the job so much he could come home. Now she put both hands on the wheel and felt it give a fraction and catch.

Behind them, her brother called out from the dugout. He wanted her to know he’d found a grasshopper. It was such an ordinary thing to hear him say. For a moment she could even hear the grin in his voice. Ithuriel moved closer to her. The sword’s point dragged a line through the wet cinders.

“May I borrow it?” Mina asked.

He held it out by the blade. She wedged it through the wheel and leaned against the hilt. The silver bent. The wheel turned far enough that she could take it the rest of the way with both hands. Under the field came the sound of water falling a great distance into an empty room.

For a little while Haru’s voice continued to come from the dugout. The last thing it said was her name. From underground, very faintly, she heard three knocks against a pipe. She struck the wheel three times with the sword. She waited with her mouth open in the rain. No more knocks came.

Ithuriel had lain down beside the wheelchair. He was struggling for breath. Mina spread his coat over him, tucking it around the shoulders as Haru must have done when he put him in the chair.

“Would you like me to stay?” she asked.

“Yes,” he said, so quietly she thought he had only moved his lips. She sat beside him until the water came through the fence.

At first light the ferryman found Mina standing waist-deep at the mainland end of the causeway. She had followed the submerged concrete by the feel of it under her feet. She brought the sword aboard wrapped in a piece of the angel’s coat. Its edge had gone black. Behind her, the lights on Kurohama were out, and the waves broke on the island without stopping.

The ferryman put a cup of tea in front of her in the wheelhouse. He had a thermos like Haru’s, only blue. The radio was off. He asked whether they needed to go back for anyone. Mina tried to say her brother’s name. The ferryman waited, his hand on the throttle. When she managed it he asked her to say it once more, so he would remember who she’d gone for.

She said it again. By then they had drifted far enough out to sea that she could see the rain coming down all along the island. The ferryman opened a tin and put two biscuits on the rim of her saucer. She ate one and slipped the other into her coat pocket. She could feel it there all the way home.
